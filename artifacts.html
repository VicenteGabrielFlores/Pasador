Los papeles, no la novela.

What the Paper Keeps

Lo que guarda el papel

A priest in Cuautitlán dipped his pen
and called them wanderers, travelers,
islanders with salt still in their names.
He did not know he was writing to me.

In San Antonio a hand pressed hard
enough to sign a petition into history,
the letters leaning like men against the wind.

At Carrizal the ink says twenty-five.
The bones say otherwise. I keep them both.
A family is what survives its clerks.

Then death in a clean bureaucratic hand,
then a draft card: a height, a weight, the color of his eyes,
the bridge crossed once, and never crossed back the same.

Un cura en Cuautitlán mojó la pluma
y los llamó andantes, pasajeros.
No sabía que me escribía a mí.

Lo que el papel no guarda, lo guardo yo.

These are records behind the work, not the work itself. A verified record is an original or an official text that has been read. Family narrative is what the Chronicles and family memory say, and it stays labeled as that. Fiction is the dialogue and the scenes in the novel and the plays: a document does not rewrite a scene, and a scene is not offered here as a record.

The papers are not all one story. They show grants and baptisms, signed letters and death certificates. They also show conflict: crown and state, the church, the colonists, and the Native peoples already living on this land. Settling new country was discovery and disruption at the same time. It was innovative, great, and awful, depending on who is looking. The settlers conquered, lived that life, and left paper that carries both the fact of conquering peoples and the richness of the land and its water, grass, and ore. The page says that plainly. Judgment belongs to the reader.

These are the papers behind the work: the Canary Island founding, Gaspar’s San Antonio, the Stephenson ranch at Concordia, and the Juárez household that crossed the bridge in 1913.

  • Verified record Registro verificado. The text has been read, or it is a printed official copy of a statute or deed.
  • Catalog record Registro de catálogo. A finding aid names the item. The instrument itself has not been read, and nothing inside it is quoted.
  • Family narrative Narrativa familiar. What the family account says. It is kept, and it is not dressed up as a certificate.

I

Travieso / Canary Islands

Travieso · Islas Canarias

Later historical writing calls this founding company Canary Islanders (Isleños). The 1730 marriage register is shown. The two wills remain cataloged and unread. The register itself says “naturales de las Islas Canarias,” and it does not use the word isleños.

15 October 1730, Pueblo de Cuautitlán

Marriage of Vicente Álvarez Travieso and Ana María Ramírez Curbelo

The San Buenaventura marriage register records that on 15 October 1730, in the Pueblo de Cuautitlán, Vicente Álvarez Travieso and Ana María Ramírez Curbelo were married, and that they were “naturales de las Islas Canarias, vagos, viandantes.” The entry does not use the word isleños. Later family telling has given other days in September; this page follows the date on this register and does not rewrite the novel.

This is the Cuautitlán marriage the Second Chronicle turns on: Vicente and Ana María Ramírez Curbelo, recorded as natives of the Canary Islands, on the road to Béxar.

II

Flores of San Antonio

Flores de San Antonio

A signed petition of November 1801 is now shown. Four letters from Gaspar Flores to the alcalde of Laredo are described and linked. The scans are not hosted here. The Portal to Texas History states general terms, not an item-level license, and that review is still open.

November 1801, San Fernando de Béxar

Petition of Gaspar Flores to marry Luisa Pérez

A matrimonial petition before the cura of San Fernando shows Gaspar Flores, called español, originario of this presidio, legitimate son of Vicente Flores and Antonia Fuentes, seeking to marry Luisa Pérez, española, originaria of this presidio, legitimate daughter of Antonio Pérez and María Romero. The page carries the signature and rubric of Gaspar Mª Flores, and his father Vicente Flores signs his consent. The day in November is not clear enough to state as a fact. A later margin date of a finished marriage is not legible enough here. This is a signed petition, not a marriage certificate, and it is not used as proof of the unread 1837 estate-file title.

This signed petition is the San Antonio paper in which Gaspar, son of Vicente Flores, asks to marry Luisa Pérez; it is the generation that stands between the Travieso founding and the later Concordia line.

Citation: FamilySearch, San Fernando de Béxar, film 008085854, the November 1801 petition (ark 3:1:3Q9M-CSNP-89DM-M). Image: FamilySearch, shared by the author.

III

Stephenson / Azcárate of El Paso

Stephenson · Azcárate de El Paso

The Ysleta pueblo grant and El Rancho de Ascarete are both printed statutes. They are shown the same way: the page, the date, the credit, and what the act does and does not prove. The name appears in these papers as Azcárate, as Ascarate, and, in the 1858 act, as Ascarete.

Approved 11 February 1858

Texas confirmation of El Rancho de Ascarete

Chapter 120 of the Seventh Legislature relinquishes the state’s right in three leagues called El Rancho de Ascarete to Juan and Jacinto Ascarete, as the act spells the name, their heirs and assigns, to be surveyed and patented, and it preserves rights that already existed.

This is the Texas paper behind the El Paso ranch the Chronicles give to Juana’s people; the act names Juan and Jacinto Ascarete, and does not, by itself, prove her kinship.

Citation: H. P. N. Gammel, The Laws of Texas, vol. 4, p. 1027. General Land Office file locator BEX 1–001390, surname index, letter A. Credit: UNT Libraries, The Portal to Texas History.

Verified record Show image Public-domain statute

Approved 31 January 1854

Texas relinquishment to the inhabitants of Ysleta

After the Rio Grande changed course in 1831 or 1832, chapter XXX relinquishes about two leagues on the east bank, formerly of the Cinecue town tract, to the inhabitants of Ysleta, and that bank was later treated as the dividing line with the Ascarate survey.

This is the pueblo grant on the other bank of the same river, the title later courts would set against the Ascarate survey, in the same El Paso country as Concordia.

Citation: Gammel, vol. 4, p. 42 and p. 43. Credit: UNT Libraries, The Portal to Texas History. A later act recites the date as 21 January 1854. That conflict is in the printed laws.

15 February 1858

Amendment of the Ysleta relinquishment

The 1858 amendment changes the 1854 relinquishment, and the printed laws recite the original approval as 21 January 1854, which does not match the 31 January date on the 1854 act.

A later session changed the 1854 relinquishment; it belongs to the same river-boundary story as the ranch act and the later lawsuits.

Citation: Gammel, vol. 4, pp. 1094–1095. Credit: UNT Libraries, The Portal to Texas History.

Verified record Show image Public-domain statute

16 November 1854

Reciprocal Bracito deeds: García y San Juan and Hugh Stephenson

Printed official copies show Francisco García y San Juan conveying two-thirds of Bracito, recited as 13,462 acres more or less, to Hugh Stephenson for $1,000, and Stephenson releasing the remaining third, recited as 6,731 acres more or less, back to García after a court partition. Bracito was not an original grant to Hugh.

These are the printed deeds by which Hugh Stephenson took two-thirds of Bracito and released the rest; they are the ranch papers of the Concordia household in PASADOR and the Fifth Chronicle, and they show the tract was not an original grant to him.

Citation: U.S. House Report 321, 36th Congress, 1st session (1860), University of Oklahoma, SHAREOK. Acknowledged 17 November 1854 and recorded 12 March 1855. Credit: University of Oklahoma Libraries, SHAREOK.

Verified record Show image Public-domain report

5 March 1914, rehearing 23 April 1914; appeal 24 February 1916

Stevens v. Crosby and Crosby v. Stevens

The opinions recite the 1854 Ysleta act, the 1858 Ascarate act, the 1886 Randolph survey, and a patent of 14 September 1886 to Juan and Jacinto Ascarate, and they hold that the 1854 bank of the Río Viejo, not later course-and-distance calls, is the dividing line.

The later opinions decide where the Ysleta grant meets El Rancho de Ascarete along the old river; they are the courtroom end of the land story that begins in the 1854 and 1858 acts.

Citation: 166 S.W. 62, Stevens v. Crosby and Crosby v. Stevens. Credit: the opinions are public. Editorial headnotes are not copied. The parties are later title holders, not proved here to be Azcárate heirs, and the 1886 patent date is taken from the opinion, not from an inspection of General Land Office file BEX 1–001390.

Flores de Juárez y El Paso

The facts below are approved for the prose of this site. The images below are shown because the author shared them, with credit to FamilySearch; they remain the archives’ records.

Died 24 June 1914, El Paso. Certificate no. 12214.

Death certificate of Margarita Stephenson de Flores

The certificate names her father Hugo Stephenson, born in Kentucky, and her mother Juana Azcarate, born in Juárez, records her as widowed, and supports a birth in 1831 in El Paso rather than 1836.

This is the last official paper of Hugh and Juana’s daughter, who in the Chronicles is the bride of the second convergence, the 1858 marriage that joined San Antonio to Concordia.

3 October 1888, Santa Iglesia de San Fernando, El Carrizal, Chihuahua

Marriage of José J. Flores and Josefa Samaniego

The register, headed “En esta Santa Yglesia de San Fernando,” records José J. Flores, called del Paso del Norte, age 25, son of José Ma. Born 10 October 1903, Ciudad Juárez. Died March 1988, El Paso, age 84.

Carlos F. Flores, draft registration and death

His World War II draft card, signed Carlos F. Flores and registered in El Paso on 16 February 1942, gives his birth as 10 October 1903 in Ciudad Juárez and names Mrs. J. Flores de Samaniego at 1519 Montana as the person who would always know his address. In factual prose, on that date, he was ten at the November 1913 crossing. The plays and the remembered family telling keep their own ages.

The death, March 1988, El Paso, age 84, follows the El Paso Times of 22 March 1988 together with the family tree already confirmed by Gabriel. The exact day is not taken from a certificate read here. The 1950 census lists this same household with Carlos F. as age 35, which is not used to overturn the signed card, and is held. The Chronicles still also list an October 1988 obituary, and an age of 85 has been stated in that family account; that conflict is held.

The signed card is the adult paper of the boy who crossed with his father in 1913, the grandfather in whose lap, as La Toma says, the history was learned.

Conflicts in the record

Conflictos en los papeles

This section is a board of conflicts, not a hidden footnote. The captions stay short. The sources are named. The grants, the water, the church, the colonists, and the Native towns are all on the paper. A pueblo grant and a private ranch grant that meet at the same riverbank are shown the same way. Where the documents do not give a Native voice, none is invented.

Principal papers 2 November 1734. Index span 1719 to 2 February 1735.

Settlers’ letters to the viceroy, with copies of royal edicts

A cataloged dossier of about twenty pages holds settlers’ letters and copies of royal edicts about water, imprisonment, refusal of medical travel, and a priest’s fees, and Vicente is not named on this index’s list of persons.

These unread letters are the kind of viceregal paper the First Play’s water fight is built from.

Citation: Archivo General de la Nación, México, Provincias Internas, vol. 236, ff. 244–253, indexed at the University of Arizona. Credit: AGN, via the Documentary Relations of the Southwest. No image is attached to the opened catalog record.

13 October 1735

Anzures and the viceroy: settlers’ land and water

Representatives including Vizente Álvarez Travieso, alguacil mayor, ask the viceroy to stop the governor’s sale of the settlers’ lands and water rights and to release prisoners.

Travieso’s name appears among those asking the viceroy to stop the sale of the settlers’ land and water; that is the historical conflict Play One dramatizes, not a scene invented from this unread file.

Documents 1771–1783. English translations 1927.

Protests against mission land claims

Vicente and Juan Andrés Álvarez Travieso protest mission land claims in papers that the Handbook of Texas connects with Rancho de las Mulas, a conflict between colonists and the missions over land, kept here separate from the water fight of the 1730s, and Native testimony is not in hand until the file is read.

Later in his life Vicente protested mission claims on land, a different fight from the 1730s water war, and one the Chronicles also keep in the founding generation.

River change recited as 1831 or 1832. Acts of 1854 and 1858. Appeals of 1914 and 1916.

Río Viejo: the Ysleta grant and El Rancho de Ascarete

The 1854 act says the inhabitants of Ysleta lost part of a grant from the government of Spain when the Rio Grande moved, leaving part of the Cinecue town tract on the east bank, and the 1914 opinion holds that the 1854 bank of the Río Viejo, not later measurements, divides that tract from the Ascarate survey.

The river that moved is the same line the courts later drew between Ysleta and the Ascarate ranch, in the country of Concordia and the later El Paso chronicles.
