Los papeles, no la novela.

Primary documentary evidence. The verified chronicle is Las Crónicas. The dramatized books are The Harness-Maker’s Son, PASADOR, and CONCORDIA: la heredera.

Move in time: 1730 to 2026Moverse en el tiempo

What the Paper Keeps

Lo que guarda el papel

A priest in Cuautitlán dipped his pen
and called them wanderers, travelers,
islanders with salt still in their names.
He did not know he was writing to me.

In San Antonio a hand pressed hard
enough to sign a petition into history,
the letters leaning like men against the wind.

At Carrizal the ink says twenty-five.
The bones say otherwise. I keep them both.
A family is what survives its clerks.

Then death in a clean bureaucratic hand,
then a draft card: a height, a weight, the color of his eyes,
the bridge crossed once, and never crossed back the same.

Un cura en Cuautitlán mojó la pluma
y los llamó andantes, pasajeros.
No sabía que me escribía a mí.

Lo que el papel no guarda, lo guardo yo.

These are records behind the work, not the work itself. Fiction is the dialogue and the scenes in the novel and the plays: a document does not rewrite a scene, and a scene is not offered here as a record.

The papers are not all one story. They show grants and baptisms, signed letters and death certificates. They also show conflict: crown and state, the church, the colonists, and the Native peoples already living on this land. Settling new country was discovery and disruption at the same time. It was innovative, great, and awful, depending on who is looking. The settlers conquered, lived that life, and left paper that carries both the fact of conquering peoples and the richness of the land and its water, grass, and ore. The page says that plainly. Judgment belongs to the reader.

The page runs from the Canary Island founding through Gaspar’s San Antonio, the Stephenson ranch at Concordia, and the Juárez household that crossed the bridge in 1913, and then to the maps and plates of Coahuila y Tejas.

How to read the labels · Cómo leer las etiquetas

  • Verified recordRegistro verificado. An original or an official text that has been read, or a printed official copy of a statute or deed.
  • Catalog recordRegistro de catálogo. A finding aid names the item. The instrument itself has not been read, and nothing inside it is quoted.
  • Family narrativeNarrativa familiar. What the Chronicles and family memory say, and what the family account says. It stays labeled as that. It is kept, and it is not dressed up as a certificate.

Travieso / Canary Islands

Travieso · Islas Canarias

Later historical writing calls this founding company Canary Islanders (Isleños). The 1730 marriage register is shown. The two wills remain cataloged and unread.

Verified record Registro verificado

15 October 1730, Pueblo de Cuautitlán

Marriage of Vicente Álvarez Travieso and Ana María Ramírez Curbelo

The San Buenaventura marriage register records that on 15 October 1730, in the Pueblo de Cuautitlán, Vicente Álvarez Travieso and Ana María Ramírez Curbelo were married, and that they were “naturales de las Islas Canarias, vagos, viandantes” — natives of the Canary Islands, wanderers, travelers. The entry does not use the word isleños. Later family telling has given other days in September; this page follows the date on this register and does not rewrite the novel.

This is the Cuautitlán marriage the Second Chronicle turns on: Vicente and Ana María Ramírez Curbelo, recorded as natives of the Canary Islands, on the road to Béxar.

Citation: FamilySearch, San Buenaventura, Cuautitlán, Matrimonios, the 15 October 1730 entry (ark 3:1:939X-QBHQ-H). Credit: the parish register. Image: FamilySearch, shared by the author.

In the Chronicles he is the alguacil mayor of the new villa, the man who kept filing for water; this cataloged will is the last paper of that life.

Author's letter, 2026

Carta del autor, 2026

Contemporary document · 2026 Documento contemporáneo · 2026

Written in the custom of Vicente Álvarez Travieso's 1734 petition.

Escrita a la manera de la petición de Vicente Álvarez Travieso en 1734.

Sent to Governor-elect Hinojosa's transition team, November 18, 2026, four days after the first Starship catch. The inauguration it names is January 19, 2027.

Dear Governor-elect Hinojosa:

In November of 1734, in the villa of San Fernando de Béxar, a Canary Islander named Vicente Álvarez Travieso asked his governor for permission to go to Saltillo. He was sick, there was no doctor within reach, and he could not leave the province without the governor's license. The governor refused him. His neighbors signed their names in protest, and the case went up to the viceroy in Mexico City. Fourteen years later Travieso stood in Mexico City himself, pressing for the money the Crown had ordered for the villa's church, and telling the viceroy, plainly, that the sum would not be enough.

My family traces its line to that man, and I write to you in his custom: one citizen, one petition, one request.

On Saturday, November 14, a Starship that had circled the Earth fourteen times came home to Starbase, at the southern tip of Texas, and the tower that had launched it closed its arms and caught it. It was the first time the ship itself had come home that way. Travieso needed a governor's permission to travel more than three hundred miles. The children now in our schools may travel farther than anyone before them, and some of the permissions they will need are signed in Austin.

Your transition has invited Texans to weigh in with their ideas. Here is mine. Before your inauguration on January 19, please convene a small working group on space and invention, made up of engineers, the Texas Space Commission, and the families who live beside the launch pads. Give it a single charge: to name each permit and rule in the State's own hands that slows a Texan who wants to build, test, or fly, and to report which of them can be made simpler and faster without making anyone less safe, and to deliver that list to you within your first hundred days.

Travieso and his neighbors asked for liberty in small, hard things: to travel, to sell their harvest, to be healed, to raise a church. I ask for this one for my sons. The youngest is still learning to stand. When he is grown and looks up, I would like the permission to already be written.

With respect, and with hope for your success,

Vicente Gabriel Flores
Austin, Texas

Enviada al equipo de transición de la gobernadora electa Hinojosa el 18 de noviembre de 2026, cuatro días después de la primera captura de la nave Starship. La toma de posesión que menciona será el 19 de enero de 2027.

Traducción; el original en inglés es el texto enviado.

Estimada gobernadora electa Hinojosa:

En noviembre de 1734, en la villa de San Fernando de Béxar, un isleño canario llamado Vicente Álvarez Travieso pidió a su gobernador permiso para ir a Saltillo. Estaba enfermo, no había médico a su alcance, y no podía salir de la provincia sin la licencia del gobernador. El gobernador se lo negó. Sus vecinos firmaron en protesta, y el caso se elevó al virrey en la Ciudad de México. Catorce años después, Travieso se presentó él mismo en la Ciudad de México, reclamando el dinero que la Corona había ordenado para la iglesia de la villa, y diciéndole al virrey, sin rodeos, que la suma no bastaría.

Mi familia traza su línea hasta aquel hombre, y le escribo a usted como él acostumbraba: un ciudadano, una petición, una solicitud.

El sábado 14 de noviembre, un Starship que había dado catorce vueltas a la Tierra volvió a casa, a Starbase, en la punta sur de Texas, y la torre que lo había lanzado cerró los brazos y lo atrapó. Fue la primera vez que la nave misma volvió a casa de ese modo. Travieso necesitaba el permiso de un gobernador para viajar más de trescientas millas. Los niños que hoy están en nuestras escuelas podrán viajar más lejos que nadie antes que ellos, y algunos de los permisos que necesitarán se firman en Austin.

Su equipo de transición ha invitado a los tejanos a aportar sus ideas. Aquí está la mía. Antes de su toma de posesión el 19 de enero, le pido que convoque un pequeño grupo de trabajo sobre el espacio y la invención, integrado por ingenieros, la Texas Space Commission y las familias que viven junto a las plataformas de lanzamiento. Dele un solo encargo: nombrar cada permiso y cada norma que esté en las propias manos del Estado y que retrase a un tejano que quiera construir, probar o volar, e informar cuáles de ellos pueden simplificarse y agilizarse sin restarle seguridad a nadie, y entregarle esa lista dentro de sus primeros cien días.

Travieso y sus vecinos pidieron libertad en cosas pequeñas y duras: viajar, vender su cosecha, curarse, levantar una iglesia. Esta la pido por mis hijos. El más pequeño todavía está aprendiendo a ponerse de pie. Cuando sea grande y mire hacia arriba, quisiera que el permiso ya estuviera escrito.

Con respeto, y con la esperanza de que tenga éxito,

Vicente Gabriel Flores
Austin, Texas

Flores of San Antonio

Flores de San Antonio

A signed petition of November 1801 is now shown. Four letters from Gaspar Flores to the alcalde of Laredo are described and linked. The scans are not hosted here. The Portal to Texas History states general terms, not an item-level license, and that review is still open.

In the Fourth Chronicle, Gaspar is the political voice of the Bexareños in Mexican Texas; these letters show him as an official of Béxar, signing ordinary business to the alcalde of Laredo.

Verified record Registro verificado

November 1801, San Fernando de Béxar

Petition of Gaspar Flores to marry Luisa Pérez

A matrimonial petition before the cura of San Fernando shows Gaspar Flores, called español, originario of this presidio, legitimate son of Vicente Flores and Antonia Fuentes, seeking to marry Luisa Pérez, española, originaria of this presidio, legitimate daughter of Antonio Pérez and María Romero. The page carries the signature and rubric of Gaspar Mª Flores, and his father Vicente Flores signs his consent. Vicente’s line begins “Digo yo Vicente Flores, que doy mi libre consentimiento” — “I, Vicente Flores, give my free consent.” The day in November is not clear enough to state as a fact. A later margin date of a finished marriage is not legible enough here. This is a signed petition, not a marriage certificate, and it is not used as proof of the unread 1837 estate-file title.

This signed petition is the San Antonio paper in which Gaspar, son of Vicente Flores, asks to marry Luisa Pérez; it is the generation that stands between the Travieso founding and the later Concordia line.

Stephenson / Azcárate of El Paso

Stephenson · Azcárate de El Paso

The Ysleta pueblo grant and El Rancho de Ascarete are both printed statutes. They are shown the same way: the page, the date, the credit, and what the act does and does not prove. The name appears in these papers as Azcárate, as Ascarate, and, in the 1858 act, as Ascarete.

Verified record Registro verificado

Approved 11 February 1858

Texas confirmation of El Rancho de Ascarete

Chapter 120 of the Seventh Legislature relinquishes the state’s right in three leagues called El Rancho de Ascarete to Juan and Jacinto Ascarete, as the act spells the name, their heirs and assigns, to be surveyed and patented, and it preserves rights that already existed. The page is Gammel’s 1898 reprint of the statute, not the original Spanish crown grant and not a deed to Juana María Ascárate. It does not by itself prove that this Juan was Juana’s father, or that Jacinto was her brother or her son.

This is the Texas paper behind the El Paso ranch the Chronicles give to Juana’s people.

Citation: H. P. N. Gammel, The Laws of Texas, vol. 4, p. 1027. General Land Office file locator BEX 1–001390, surname index, letter A. Credit: UNT Libraries, The Portal to Texas History.

Public-domain statute
Verified record Registro verificado

Approved 31 January 1854

Texas relinquishment to the inhabitants of Ysleta

The river's change of channel from 1829 to 1831 left Ysleta on the north side, and from 1848 the pueblo was in Texas. The 1854 act recites that move as 1831 or 1832. Chapter XXX relinquishes about two leagues on the east bank, formerly of the Cinecue town tract, to the inhabitants of Ysleta, and that bank was later treated as the dividing line with the Ascarate survey. Ysleta del Sur was a Pueblo community, and the act recites a Spanish grant. El río, al mudar de cauce entre 1829 y 1831, dejó a Ysleta en la margen norte, y desde 1848 el pueblo estuvo en Tejas.

This is the pueblo grant on the other bank of the same river, the title later courts would set against the Ascarate survey, in the same El Paso country as Concordia.

Citation: Gammel, vol. 4, p. 42 and p. 43. Credit: UNT Libraries, The Portal to Texas History. A later act recites the date as 21 January 1854. That conflict is in the printed laws.

Verified record Registro verificado

15 February 1858

Amendment of the Ysleta relinquishment

The 1858 amendment changes the 1854 relinquishment, and the printed laws recite the original approval as 21 January 1854, which does not match the 31 January date on the 1854 act. The pages are the printed certified text of the statute, not a manuscript grant.

It belongs to the same river-boundary story as the ranch act and the later lawsuits.

Verified record Registro verificado

16 November 1854

Reciprocal Bracito deeds: García y San Juan and Hugh Stephenson

Printed certified copies in the 1860 House report, not the autograph deeds or the court’s original paper, show Francisco García y San Juan conveying two-thirds of Bracito, recited as 13,462 acres more or less, to Hugh Stephenson for $1,000, and Stephenson releasing the remaining third, recited as 6,731 acres more or less, back to García after a court partition. Bracito was not an original grant to Hugh.

These are the ranch papers of the Concordia household in PASADOR and the Fifth Chronicle.

Verified record Registro verificado

5 March 1914, rehearing 23 April 1914; appeal 24 February 1916

Stevens v. Crosby and Crosby v. Stevens

The opinions recite the 1854 Ysleta act, the 1858 Ascarate act, the 1886 Randolph survey, and a patent of 14 September 1886 to Juan and Jacinto Ascarate, and they hold that the 1854 bank of the Río Viejo, not later course-and-distance calls, is the dividing line.

They are the courtroom end of the land story that begins in the 1854 and 1858 acts.

Flores de Juárez y El Paso

Verified record Registro verificado

Died 24 June 1914, El Paso. Certificate no. 12214.

Death certificate of Margarita Stephenson de Flores

The certificate names her father Hugo Stephenson, born in Kentucky, and her mother Juana Azcarate, born in Juárez, records her as widowed, and supports a birth in 1831 in El Paso rather than 1836.

This is the last official paper of Hugh and Juana’s daughter, who in the Chronicles is the bride of the second convergence, the 1858 marriage that joined San Antonio to Concordia.

Verified record Registro verificado

3 October 1888, Santa Iglesia de San Fernando, El Carrizal, Chihuahua

Marriage of José J. Flores and Josefa Samaniego

The register opens “En esta Santa Yglesia de San Fernando, á los tres días del mes de Octubre” — “In this Holy Church of San Fernando, on the third day of October.” It records José J. Flores, called del Paso del Norte, age 25, son of José Ma. Flores and Margarita Stephenson, marrying Josefa Samaniego, daughter of Fernando Samaniego and Guadalupe Siqueiros, in the Pueblo del Carrizal. The image is cropped to this entry so that the other marriages on the same opening are not shown. The last witness, Ygnacio Domínguez, bachelor, twenty-six, stands on the facing leaf and is named here, not pictured. Age 25 on this date conflicts with the 24 December 1861 birth on his death certificate, and both readings are held.

This is the Carrizal marriage of the man the later plays follow as alcalde, José J. Flores, son of José María and Margarita, to Josefa Samaniego; it is the household from which Carlos would come.

Carlos F. Flores, draft registration and death

His World War II draft card, signed Carlos F. Flores and registered in El Paso on 16 February 1942, gives his birth as 10 October 1903 in Ciudad Juárez and names Mrs. J. Flores de Samaniego at 1519 Montana as the person who would always know his address. In factual prose, on that date, he was ten at the November 1913 crossing. The plays and the remembered family telling keep their own ages.

The Chronicles still also list an October 1988 obituary, and an age of 85 has been stated in that family account; that conflict is held.

Río Viejo: the Ysleta grant and El Rancho de Ascarete

The 1854 act says the inhabitants of Ysleta lost part of a grant from the government of Spain when the Rio Grande moved, leaving part of the Cinecue town tract on the east bank, and the 1914 opinion holds that the 1854 bank of the Río Viejo, not later measurements, divides that tract from the Ascarate survey.

The river that moved is the same line the courts later drew between Ysleta and the Ascarate ranch, in the country of Concordia and the later El Paso chronicles.
