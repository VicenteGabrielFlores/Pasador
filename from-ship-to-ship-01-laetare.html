From Ship to Ship / De navío a navío

Play I — Laetare

Vicente Gabriel Flores

por Vicente Gabriel Flores

A play in five scenes. Garachico, Tenerife. One day and one night, the fourth Sunday of Lent, March 19, 1730.

Part history, part imagination. Names marked † are in the historical record; their words, their nights and their feelings here are imagined. For adult readers.

THE PEOPLE OF THE PLAY

Grouped by household and rank, as a ship's manifest would group them. † marks a person in the historical record.

The house of the harness-maker, Calle de abajo, above the old port

  • † VICENTE ÁLVAREZ TRAVIESO, 24. Harness-maker, his father's son and now the better hand at the bench. Deep in the chest, not tall, hands too large for any glove in Garachico. Speaks little, and when he does, in short plain sentences that end before you expect them to. Smells of beeswax, neat's-foot oil and the horse-sweat that never leaves a saddler's cuffs. Wants to go somewhere there is no wall yet.

    Documented: born 1705 in Tenerife, son of Juan and Catarina (Cayetano) Álvarez Travieso; joined the 1730 Canary Islander migration and married at Cuautitlán that autumn (TSHA). The King's list at Cuautitlán that November gives him as twenty-five; twenty-four in March is consistent if his birthday falls after March 19. Invented: everything he says and does in this play, and his Garachico trade and lost love, which come from Gabriel's novel The Harness-Maker's Son.

  • † CATARINA CAYETANO, his mother, about 50. Gray eyes that go green at a window. A mender, a counter of coins, a woman who knows things before they happen and does not say them. Small, quick, a little stooped from thirty years of needlework. Smells of lye soap and rosemary.

    Documented: named as Vicente's mother (TSHA). Invented: her age, voice, and her presence in this play.

  • JUAN ÁLVAREZ TRAVIESO, his father, past 50. Harness-maker. A forward stoop from the bench that will not straighten now. Says less than his son. When he speaks it is to his tools.

    Named as Vicente's father (TSHA). He does NOT appear on the Nov 8, 1730 Cuautitlán enrollment; his going on the voyage is family narrative, not documented.

The neighbors

  • PANCHA LA DEL PESCADO (Francisca Gorrín), 52. Fishwife, widow, the street's living newspaper. Wide hips, forearms like a stevedore's, three teeth on top. Salt crust in the creases of her knuckles; she smells of sardine and of the clove she chews to hide it. Calls everyone mi niño or mi niña, including the priest. Wants Catarina's copper cauldron for half of what it's worth, and wants Vicente to take her grown son Blas, 20, to the Indies with him, so that one Gorrín will eat meat twice a week before he dies.

  • ANDRÉS PERAZA, 31. The young saddler buying the Travieso shop for a price that is an insult and a kindness both. Clean hands, a new hat, a cough he says is nothing. Wants the shop, and wants Juan's half-moon knife thrown into the bargain, which it will not be.

The house of Bençomo, the coat of arms over the door on the Calle Real

  • INÉS BENÇOMO, 24. Daughter of a name, married this past autumn to a widower up the coast. Mouth too wide for her face, a nose with an opinion of its own, dark even for the island, and a laugh louder than a Bençomo is allowed. A good black dress, turned once already at the hem. Rosewater at her throat; under it, faintly, the camphor of her husband's sickroom, which she cannot wash out. Wants, tonight, one thing that is hers.

  • SEBASTIANA, 37. Inés's maid since Inés was in skirts. Freckled, big-boned, a widow herself; a burn scar on one forearm from a kitchen fire. Calls her mistress señora, in company and out of it, and has since the wedding; what she called her before, she keeps to herself. Carries messages and lies with the same flat face. Wants her eleven months of back wages before her mistress does anything foolish, because after a scandal nobody pays the maid, and she says so.

  • DON BALTASAR BENÇOMO, 30. Inés's brother. Keeps the family name like a man guarding an empty strongbox. Heard, not seen, until the last scene.

Lucía's venta, below the church of Santa Ana

  • LUCÍA, 41. Keeps the venta, the tavern-inn where the north coast drinks. A widow twice. Big, handsome, breasts she props on the counter when she is tired and on her folded arms when she is listening. Gold hoops, a gap between her front teeth, a laugh that comes up from the floor. Runs the room with a dish-rag and an eyebrow. Wants her best customers not to be leaving on a ship, and wants, tonight, after twenty-two years of saying no, to stop saying it.

  • MELCHOR, about 60. Once a ship's carpenter, now the man who walks the drunk home. Iron-gray beard that was red once. A bad knee, a good laugh, hands as scarred as Vicente's. Has loved Lucía since before her second husband and has never once said so out loud. Wants to be asked to stay.

  • TOMÁS, called TOMASITO, 33. Lucía's tapster, built like a wine pipe, gentle as a calf. Wants the guitar to be his tonight and not the Gomeran's.

  • PADRE ANSELMO RIVERO, 58. A beneficiado of Santa Ana. Thin, a drip at the nose in all weathers, a weakness for malvasía that he confesses every Saturday and resumes every Sunday. Wants the venta quiet in Lent, and wants a cup.

  • MARCELA, called LA RONCA, about 46. Washerwoman at the town fountain, widow of a boatman, with a voice like a cracked jug that still holds water. Her grown son Agustín shipped to Havana on a wine ship six years ago and has not written. Wants someone going to the Indies to ask for him.

  • NICOLÁS DÍAZ, 23. A boatman's son with a new mustache and his first good jacket. Sings at Lucía every Sunday. Wants her to laugh at one of his songs; tonight she laughs at Marcela's.

  • BLAS GORRÍN, 20. Pancha's son. A good back, a patched jacket, his mother's mouth and none of her words. Wants to be asked nothing and taken anyway.

  • A GOMERAN WITH A GUITAR, DRINKERS, AN ARRIERO (a mule-driver from Santa Cruz).

Production note on speech. Every island character speaks with seseo (c and z sound as s): wills drawn up at La Guancha, next door, were spelling sinco and setesientos in 1729–35. The Spanish lines keep standard spelling; the actors carry the sound. Address follows Research's 1730 check: the respect form, written vuestra merced, is ordinarily said as usted; when Vicente says the full vuestra merced at the pools he is choosing the stiffest form there is, and Inés laughs at it (a dramatist's choice). It is rendered "Your Mercy"; tú for intimates, equals and those beneath you; vos only from a superior to an inferior, in anger; plural vosotros/os, never ustedes for everyone. No isa, no timple, no mojo, guagua or enchumbar: none is attested by 1730.

PROLOGUE

A bare stage. A bell, once.

CHORUS

Garachico, on the north coast of Tenerife.
The fourth Sunday of Lent, the nineteenth of March, in the year 1730.
Nearly twenty-four years since the mountain filled the harbor with stone.
Two days' walk over the island, at Santa Cruz, a ship rides at anchor for the King.

SCENE ONE — The Shop

Afternoon. The workshop below the Travieso rooms. Walls bare where the tools hung; pale shapes on the whitewash where they were. A bench, a chest, a copper cauldron, a rolled bundle of oiled leather on the floor. Dust in the slant light, and the smell that will outlast them all in this room: tanned hide, beeswax, old sweat.

JUAN kneels by the bundle, unrolling and rerolling his tools as if counting children. VICENTE lashes the chest. CATARINA sits at the window with a stocking, not mending it. ANDRÉS PERAZA stands in the doorway, turning his new hat.

ANDRÉS

Forty pesos, Maestro Juan, and the bench. That is my word.

JUAN (to the tools)

The bench was my father's.

ANDRÉS

Then it knows the work. It stays with the work.

JUAN

Forty-five.

ANDRÉS

I have a wife who has already sewn the curtains for the window. Forty-two, and the knife.

JUAN's hand closes on the half-moon blade. He does not look up. A breath in, held; let out through the nose.

JUAN

Forty-two. The knife goes in the water with me if the ship goes down.

ANDRÉS (coughs into his sleeve; a wet cough he pretends is dry)

God forbid it, Maestro. Forty-two.

He counts it out on the bench, coin by coin. CATARINA watches every coin. VICENTE does not watch at all; he pulls the last knot tight, and before the pull the same breath, held and let go through the nose.

CATARINA

Count it again, Andrés. Out loud. The saints are listening, and so is the street.

He counts again. Forty-two. CATARINA nods. ANDRÉS goes, coughing. The moment the door shuts, PANCHA LA DEL PESCADO comes through it without knocking, with a basket on her hip and the street on her mouth.

PANCHA

¡Ave María Purísima!

— Hail Mary most pure!

CATARINA (without warmth, without pause)

Sin pecado concebida.

— Conceived without sin.

PANCHA

Forty-two pesos. I heard it through the wall. You were robbed, comadre, and I've come to rob you worse. The cauldron. I'll give you six reales.

CATARINA

It's worth three pesos if it's worth a sardine.

PANCHA

Where you're going there's no sardines and no cauldrons either. They cook in the ground over there, they say, and eat off leaves. Six reales and a blessing.

CATARINA

And what else? You never cross a threshold for one thing.

PANCHA (sets down the basket; salt fish in it, split and stiff as shingles; the room fills with it)

My Blas. Twenty years old, and his back is good, and he's eaten nothing but gofio and fish since he had teeth. Take him on your paper, Vicente, mi niño. Say he's your cousin. Say he's a harness-maker. The King won't look in his mouth.

VICENTE

The King's man counts the families, Pancha. Not the cousins.

PANCHA

Then marry him to somebody on the boat. There are Lanzarote girls, they say, with their fathers' whole houses in one sack. (To CATARINA.) That's how it's done now. You leave your son in the bed of a stranger's daughter and call it the Indies.

JUAN (still to the tools)

Ay, Pancha.

PANCHA

Ay, Pancha nothing. Four reales for the cauldron, then, and I'll give you news for the other two.

CATARINA looks at her a long moment. Then she takes the stocking off her knee and lays it down.

CATARINA

Speak.

PANCHA

Inés Bençomo is in Garachico.

Nothing moves except VICENTE's hands, which stop. Then go on.

PANCHA (she has seen the hands)

Came in on a mule last night, with only that big freckled Sebastiana walking at the bridle. No husband. No letter, they say, or a letter nobody's seen. Don Baltasar met her at the door like a man meeting the bailiff. She's at her brother's house under that stone shield of theirs, and the old widower's up the coast in bed with his gout, and his first wife's sister is the one rubbing it. (A clove from her apron pocket; she chews.) So. That's two reales of news. The rest is free. She's thinner. And she was at the window this morning when the Ponte's mules went by, looking out like a woman who's counting the doors in a burning house.

CATARINA

That's enough.

PANCHA

It's all I have.

CATARINA

Then it's enough. (She lifts the cauldron herself, heavy, and puts it into Pancha's arms.) Take it. For nothing. And tell your Blas to go to the King's man himself, in Santa Cruz, with his own mouth. If he can't ask for his own life, he won't live it on the other side.

PANCHA, for once silent, holds the cauldron like a baby. She crosses herself with one hand, awkwardly, and goes. CATARINA sits again. She does not take up the stocking.

CATARINA

Vicente.

VICENTE

Madre.

CATARINA

We leave before the light. Your father wants the mule loaded by the second bell.

VICENTE

It will be loaded.

CATARINA

I know it will be loaded. I am not asking about the mule.

Pause. JUAN rolls the tools a final time and ties them. He stands, which takes him a while.

JUAN

A stitch you can't take out, you don't put in.

CATARINA

Juan.

JUAN

I'm talking about leather.

He goes up the stair with the bundle against his chest. VICENTE stands. He looks at the bare wall, at the pale shape where the awls hung for thirty years, and then he takes his hat.

CATARINA

Lucía's will have a guitar tonight. They'll call it Laetare and dare Anselmo to stop it. (She looks at the window, not at him.) Go and drink with Melchor. Say goodbye to the men who will be dead when you come back, which is all of them, because you aren't coming back.

VICENTE

I'm going to Lucía's.

CATARINA

I know where you're going.

He goes. She takes up the stocking. Her hands do not mend it.

SCENE TWO — Lucía's

Night. The venta below Santa Ana: one long low room, a plank counter, two wine pipes on their sides, a fire with salt cod soaking in a basin beside it, tallow candles that smoke and stink. Wet wool, garlic, the sour floor, the sweat of thirty bodies, the sweet edge of malvasía. Lenten fare on the boards: gofio in a leather bag kneaded with water, boiled papas with coarse salt, sardines on the coals, a dish of bitter olives. The GOMERAN tunes a guitar with five courses of string. TOMASITO, behind the tap, watches the guitar the way a dog watches a plate.

LUCÍA at the counter, arms folded under her breasts. MELCHOR at the end of it, his bad leg out straight on a stool. DRINKERS. VICENTE comes in and the room makes a sound, not quite a cheer: they know he is leaving.

A DRINKER

¡El hidalgo! ¡Abrid paso al hidalgo de solar conocido!

— The gentleman! Make way for the gentleman of a known house!

Laughter. A cup is pressed into VICENTE's hand. He drinks it off and gives it back. More laughter.

LUCÍA

Leave him. In a year you'll all be begging his steward for a job feeding his pigs. (To VICENTE, softer.) Sit by Melchor. He's been saving you a stool with his bad leg for an hour.

MELCHOR

Two hours. My knee's gone to sleep and taken the rest of me with it.

VICENTE sits. LUCÍA pours. Her hand on the jug is red and cracked from lye; her rings have sunk into the fingers.

MELCHOR

They say the Crown is paying the passage.

VICENTE

The Crown is paying the passage.

MELCHOR

And a year's keep. And land. And they'll write don in front of a harness-maker's name.

VICENTE

They say they'll write hijodalgo after it. The don I'd have to buy.

MELCHOR

I sailed for the King once. Eleven months to Havana and back on a ship that leaked like a nun's secret. The King paid me in biscuit with weevils in it and a promise. I'm still waiting for the promise. (He drinks.) Go anyway. Go for me. Eat something there that I never ate.

The GOMERAN begins to play, a slow turning figure in three. The room settles. TOMASITO's fingers move on the counter, playing it with him. MARCELA LA RONCA stands by the fire.

MARCELA (a folía, slow)

La mar se me lleva el hijo,
la tierra me lleva el pan;
el cura me lleva el alma,
¿y a mí quién me ha de llevar?

— The sea takes my son,
the land takes my bread;
the priest takes my soul,
and who is going to take me?

A low growl of approval from the room; somebody beats a cup on the board. Into this, from the street door, PADRE ANSELMO, with the drip at his nose and his cloak wet at the hem.

PADRE ANSELMO

In Lent. In the fourth week of Lent, Lucía. With the Host in the tabernacle not forty steps up that hill.

LUCÍA

It's Laetare, Father. Rejoice, the Mass says. I heard you say it this morning. You said it in Latin, so maybe it doesn't count down here.

PADRE ANSELMO

Laetare means the rose vestment, not the guitar.

LUCÍA

Then wear the rose vestment and sit down. (A cup, already poured, malvasía, gold in the candlelight. She slides it to the empty place beside MELCHOR.) The Traviesos are going to the Indies in the morning, and the sea takes whom it likes. Bless him and drink. God sees you with a cup anyway; better He sees you with one in good company.

PADRE ANSELMO looks at the cup. He looks at VICENTE. He sits, slowly, and wipes his nose with the back of his wrist.

PADRE ANSELMO

Un dedo. Por el viaje.

— A finger's width. For the journey.

He drinks a good deal more than a finger. The guitar goes on. The GOMERAN changes to a quicker figure, a seguidilla, and the drinkers clap it.

NICOLÁS (singing it at LUCÍA, the room joining the end of each line)

Dicen que allá en las Indias
la plata sobra;
yo no quiero la plata,
quiero tu sombra.

— They say over there in the Indies
silver is left over;
I don't want the silver,
I want your shadow.

MARCELA (answering him, bawdier, to a roar)

Tu sombra no me sirve,
que sombra es fría;
dame lo que te sobra
de noche y día.

— Your shadow's no use to me,
a shadow is cold;
give me what you've got to spare
by night and by day.

The room howls. PADRE ANSELMO covers his face with his hand, which also hides his cup. LUCÍA laughs from the floor up, the gap in her teeth shining. MELCHOR watches her laugh. VICENTE sees him watch.

NICOLÁS (to LUCÍA, a hand on his heart, grinning)

Mine was for you, Lucía.

LUCÍA

They're all for me, Nicolás. Hers was the one I laughed at. Drink your wine.

MARCELA comes past VICENTE on her way to the jug and stops, close, so the room can't hear.

MARCELA

Agustín Hernández. From here, from below the church. A sailor, with a birthmark by the left ear and my mouth. He went to Havana on the Ponte's wine six years ago. If the King's ship puts in there, ask for him on the quay. Tell him his mother is still singing, and she'd like to stop.

VICENTE

I'll ask.

MARCELA

They all say that. (She pats his cheek, hard.) You I believe. You don't say enough to lie.

VICENTE (low)

You've never told her.

MELCHOR

Told her what?

VICENTE

Twenty years.

MELCHOR

Twenty-two. (He drinks.) A man who walks the drunk home learns which doors are shut. Hers was shut for her first husband and then for her second, and when the second one died I was fifty, and a man of fifty with a knee like this one doesn't knock. He waits to be asked in out of the rain. (Beat.) And you? You're going to the end of the world without knocking either?

VICENTE does not answer. The street door opens again, a cold breath of sea, and SEBASTIANA stands in it, a shawl over her head, her burned forearm bare where she holds the shawl closed. She does not come in. She looks at the room until she finds VICENTE, then she looks at LUCÍA.

LUCÍA (without turning her head)

Tomasito. A jug of the good one for the woman at the door, who isn't here.

TOMASITO takes the jug. SEBASTIANA takes it from him on the threshold and puts it under her shawl. As VICENTE rises and passes her, going out as if to piss in the alley, she speaks without moving her lips.

SEBASTIANA

Below the castle. Where the new rocks make the pools. When the friars ring for Matins. (He stops.) Don't stop, señor, they're all looking. And if you're seen, I go unpaid. Eleven months already. (He goes on into the dark. She calls after him, for the room.) ¡Y cuidado con el charco, que está todo meado!

— And watch the puddle, the whole thing's pissed in!

Laughter behind her. She goes. LUCÍA looks at the door a long time. Then she looks at MELCHOR, who is looking at her, and for once she does not look away first.

PADRE ANSELMO (to his empty cup)

Laetare.

SCENE THREE — The Pools Below the Castle

Near midnight. The lava shore below the Castillo de San Miguel: black rock, folded and frozen where it ran into the sea in 1706, making pools that fill and empty with the swell. No moon: Laetare is a dark night, the new moon gone down behind the sun. Stars through cloud, and the white of the surf. The sea breathes in and out of the rocks with a sound like a man asleep. Cold. The smell of weed, salt, wet stone, and, faintly, from the town, smoke.

VICENTE stands with his back to the castle wall. INÉS comes down over the rocks with her skirts gathered in one fist and her shoes in the other hand, barefoot on the lava, wincing and not slowing. SEBASTIANA stays above, a dark shape on the castle steps with the jug.

INÉS

Don't help me. If you help me I'll fall, and then it'll be your fault, and then they'll say you pushed me, and I've had enough of being pushed. (She arrives. She is out of breath. She stands close.) Well. Look at me, then. Everyone else has.

VICENTE

Vuestra merced está más delgada.

— Your Mercy has grown thinner.

INÉS

"Vuestra merced." (A laugh, too loud, the Bençomo laugh she was never allowed; she covers her own mouth and laughs into her hand.) Four months a wife and I'm "Your Mercy" on a rock at midnight. You used to call me nothing at all. You used to just look, and I'd know my name.

VICENTE

You're married.

INÉS

I'm married. To a man who smells of camphor and old piss and the Gospel of Saint John, which he has read to him every night because he can no longer see the letters. He's kind. Write that down when you're a gentleman in the Indies and they ask you about me: she married a kind man. He puts his hand on my head when I bring him his broth, the way you put a hand on a dog. Twice since All Saints he has tried to be my husband. Once he fell asleep. Once he wept. (She is very still.) I held him both times. I'm not a monster. I held him until he slept.

The sea comes into the pool at their feet and goes out.

VICENTE

Why are you here, Inés?

INÉS

Because his first wife's sister wants the house, and wants me out of it before I give him an heir, and a wife who has given no heir by Easter is a wife people start to pray for out loud. Because my brother has written to him three times asking for money that was promised against my body, and the money hasn't come, and so my brother has decided my body is defective. And because you're leaving in the morning. (Beat.) Sebastiana told me the day. Sebastiana knows the day of everything. (A glance up at the dark shape on the steps.) And she told you about her wages, I suppose; she tells everyone. I told her she'd be paid in scandal like the rest of us.

VICENTE

What do you want from me?

INÉS

Take me with you.

He says nothing. The sea says something.

INÉS

No. I know. Married women don't go on the King's ship; the King's man counts the families, and I'm a family already, God help me, a family of one old man and one sister-in-law and a mule. (She comes closer.) Then the other thing.

VICENTE

What other thing.

INÉS

Give me a child.

Silence. The sea draws back off the rocks and holds there.

INÉS

Don't look at me like that, like I've put a bad stitch in your work. Every woman in this town knows how heirs get made in houses where the husband reads the Gospel of John in bed. Half the names on the pews in Santa Ana were made in the dark by somebody's cousin. The child would be his, in the book. It would have a house and land and a name over the door, and it would have your hands. You'd be in the Indies. Nobody would ever know but me, and I would know every day, and that is the thing I want. One thing in that house that's mine.

She undoes the black shawl and lets it drop on the rock. Then the lacing of her bodice, quickly, with fingers that know the knots in the dark. She pulls the bodice open and the shift beneath it down off her shoulders, and stands bare to the waist in the cold, her nipples hard with it, gooseflesh up her arms, the little light there is, starlight and the white of the breaking sea, on her collarbones and on the soft fold of her belly above the skirt. She does not cover herself.

INÉS

I was told a wife's body is for her husband and for God. My husband can't, and God hasn't asked. So I'm asking. Look at it. You looked at me across the church for a year. Look now, there's nobody to stop you.

He looks. A long time.

VICENTE I have wanted this

INÉS since the feast of the Rosary

VICENTE since the missal case,

INÉS since before,

VICENTE every night of a year

INÉS then take it. It's mine to give.

VICENTE It's yours. And I'd be gone in the morning,
and you'd be here with it.

He picks up the shawl. He does not put it around her. He holds it out, and waits, and she does not take it. So he steps in and puts his arms around her instead, bare as she is, and she presses against him through his shirt, cold skin against warm, and kisses him hard, open-mouthed, her hands in his hair, his hands spread on her bare back, a kiss with a year in it. She pulls his hand to her breast and he lets her, and holds it there, and his breath goes out through his nose, the held breath and the release, his father's gesture, before a cut that cannot be undone.

And he does not make it. He takes his hand back and puts it flat on her shoulder blade, over the shawl he finally wraps around her.

VICENTE

Tú lo pagarías sola.

— You would pay for it alone.

INÉS (her forehead against his chest, her voice wet)

I'm paying for everything alone. That's the one thing I've got plenty of.

VICENTE

Not this. Your brother would know by the color of its eyes. The sister would count the months on her fingers in the plaza. And the child would pay after you. I won't sew a thing I can't stand behind.

INÉS

"Sew." God in heaven, he's a harness-maker even with his hand on my breast. (She laughs, wet, and hits his chest once with her fist, not softly.) You'd rather be good than have me.

VICENTE

I'd rather you were alive in that house than talked to death in it.

She steps back and does up her bodice herself, quickly, crooked, the lacing skipping an eyelet. He reaches to fix it, and she slaps his hand away, and then lets him. He relaces it right, eyelet by eyelet, his big fingers sure, and ties it.

INÉS

I kept the case. The missal case. It's in his house, in my chest, with my good linen. I take it out on Sundays and put my thumb in the tooling. He thinks I'm pious.

VICENTE

You are pious.

INÉS

I'm something. (Pause. She looks at the sea, which is where he will be.) Then you'll remember me unhad. That's worse for you. I wanted it to be worse for you.

VICENTE

It is.

INÉS

Good.

From the castle steps, SEBASTIANA, urgent, low:

SEBASTIANA

Señora. Señora, the lantern. Don Baltasar's lantern on the Calle Real, coming down. He's found your bed empty and he's out in his nightshirt with the stick.

INÉS gathers her shoes. She looks at VICENTE once more, the whole look, the one he used to give her.

INÉS

Aunque la mar se seque.

— Though the sea run dry.

She does not finish the line. She goes up the rocks barefoot, cut and bleeding a little at the heel, and does not slow. SEBASTIANA takes her arm at the top, and the two women are gone into the dark of the town. VICENTE stands alone. Above, a lantern swings along the street, and a man's voice, thin, trying not to wake anyone, calls a woman's name once and then thinks better of it.

SCENE FOUR — Lucía's Room

The same night, later. The little room behind the venta: a bed with a straw tick, a crucifix with a sprig of last year's palm behind it, a chest, a basin. One candle. Through the wall, the last of the drinkers being put out by TOMASITO, a cup breaking, then quiet. The smell of the room: wine lees, lavender in the chest, tallow, a woman's warm linen.

LUCÍA sits on the bed unpinning her hair. It comes down long and gray-streaked, heavy, past her shoulder blades. MELCHOR stands in the doorway with his hat in both hands, the way he has stood in a hundred doorways at the end of the night, about to say goodnight.

MELCHOR

The Padre's home. I walked him to his door and held his head while he was sick on Saint Anne's step. He said I was a good Christian and then he said a word I didn't know priests knew.

LUCÍA

He knows them all. He hears them on Saturdays.

MELCHOR

Well. (He turns the hat.) Goodnight, Lucía.

LUCÍA

Melchor.

MELCHOR

What.

LUCÍA

It's raining.

He looks back over his shoulder at the dark yard. It is not raining.

MELCHOR

It's not raining.

LUCÍA

It's raining in here. Shut the door before you get wet.

A long beat. He shuts the door. He stays against it.

MELCHOR

Twenty-two years.

LUCÍA

I know how long it's been. I was there. Every night you stood in that door with your hat and I let you go and then I lay here and called myself a fool in Spanish and in my mother's Portuguese. My first husband beat me. My second husband was a good man who died coughing in this bed, and I swore I'd never let another man in it to die. And tonight the Traviesos are leaving, and the town's emptying out like a pipe with a knocked bung, and I thought, Lucía, the next one to go will be you, and nobody will have ever seen you with your hair down but God and the dead.

She stands, and unlaces her bodice, unhurried, and lets her skirt drop, and pulls the shift over her head, and stands naked in the candlelight: a big body, a body that has worked; heavy breasts that hang; a belly with the silver stretch-marks of a child that did not live; broad hips, strong legs with blue veins at the backs of the knees; a scar low on one side where a surgeon opened her once. She does not suck in her belly. She lifts her chin.

LUCÍA

This is what there is. Forty-one years of it.

MELCHOR puts his hat on the chest very carefully, as if it might wake someone.

MELCHOR

Mira que eres hermosa.

— Look how beautiful you are.

LUCÍA

Liar.

MELCHOR

A carpenter doesn't lie about wood. (He comes to her, limping.) You'll have to help me with the knee.

LUCÍA

I'll help you with everything. Take off that shirt, it smells of the Padre's vomit.

She undresses him, and she is not gentle with the buttons. His chest is gray-furred and thick, his belly soft, his arms still a working man's; a long white rope of scar down one shin where a spar came down on it at sea. When she gets his breeches off he looks at the ceiling.

MELCHOR (to the ceiling)

I'm not what I was.

LUCÍA (her hand on him)

You're enough. You'll be more in a minute if you stop talking.

He laughs. She draws him down onto the straw tick, which crackles and lets out a breath of old lavender, and they are clumsy, and the knee will not bend the right way, and she laughs into his neck and swings her leg over him and takes him into her herself, slowly, her hands braced on his chest, both of them gasping at it, and then still, just still, his hands on her hips, looking at each other as if they had not been in the same room every night for twenty-two years.

MELCHOR

Lucía.

LUCÍA

Don't talk. You've waited long enough to say things. Now shut up and have it.

She moves on him, and he rises to meet her, and it is not graceful and not quiet; the bed knocks the wall, the crucifix shivers on its nail, she says something to God that is not a prayer, and he says her name into her breast over and over as though he were learning to pronounce it. When she comes she grips his shoulders and laughs out loud, a big helpless laugh, the one from the floor up; he follows her a moment after with a groan like a ship's timber taking strain. They lie tangled and sweating in the cold room. The candle gutters. Through the wall, TOMASITO, who has heard everything, very softly begins to play the Gomeran's left-behind guitar.

LUCÍA (after a while, her head on his chest)

In Lent.

MELCHOR

In the fourth week of Lent.

LUCÍA

With the Host in the tabernacle not forty steps up that hill.

MELCHOR

I'll confess it Saturday.

LUCÍA

You'll confess it to Anselmo, who'll give you three Aves and ask how it was.

MELCHOR

And what will I say?

LUCÍA (she lifts her head and looks at him)

Say it was worth the wait. And then say it again Sunday, because you'll be back.

MELCHOR

Every night?

LUCÍA

Every night until one of us is dead, and then the other one can be sorry for not starting sooner.

He holds her. Through the wall the guitar finds a slow figure in three, and TOMASITO, not well, but meaning it, sings:

TOMASITO (offstage)

Aunque la mar se seque,
yo te he de querer.

— Though the sea run dry,
I'll love you still.

LUCÍA, half asleep, joins him on the last line without opening her eyes.

SCENE FIVE — The Door

Before dawn. The street outside the Travieso house. The sky over the sea going from black to the color of a bruise. Cold, wet cobbles, a smell of mule dung and woodsmoke. The mule stands loaded: the chest, the bundles, the rolled tools tied where JUAN can put his hand on them. Lanterns. JUAN checks a girth strap, then checks it again; he made the strap, and he still checks it.

CATARINA in the doorway in her good black dress and cloak, a basket on her arm: gofio, a cheese, dried figs. She looks once at the bare room behind her and closes the door on it. She does not lock it. It is not hers to lock.

VICENTE comes up the street from the sea. His shirt is wet at the shoulders. He has not slept. He does not offer a reason.

CATARINA

You smell of the sea.

VICENTE

I was at the sea.

CATARINA

Only the sea?

VICENTE

Only the sea, Madre.

She looks at him. She reads him the way she reads a hem: for what was not done. Then she reaches up and pulls a strand of a woman's long dark hair off his collar, and holds it in the lantern light between finger and thumb a moment, and lets the wind take it.

CATARINA

Bien.

— Good.

Down the street a door opens: PANCHA, in a nightcap, with the copper cauldron in her arms. The news reaches the Travieso door before she does.

PANCHA

Comadre. The Bençomo bride came back in the black of the night with her feet cut to ribbons and the maid holding her up, and before the cocks were up her brother was out in the yard with a hammer, nailing her shutters. (She looks at nobody. She looks at VICENTE.) Mother of God, the noise. Half the street heard it.

VICENTE's hands, on the mule's load, stop. Then go on.

PANCHA tries to give the cauldron back. CATARINA will not take it. PANCHA cries, loud and wet, with her whole face, and presses a twist of paper on her instead: cloves. Then, behind PANCHA, a young man in a patched jacket with a bundle on his back: BLAS, twenty, the good back, standing straight because his mother is watching him.

PANCHA

He's going to Santa Cruz to ask the King's man with his own mouth. Like you said. (To VICENTE.) If they take him, you look after him, mi niño. If they don't, you tell him he can come home and be a fool like his father.

VICENTE

If they take him, he walks with us.

BLAS (to CATARINA, too loud, because he has been practicing it)

I'll ask him myself, señora. With my own mouth.

CATARINA

Then go and ask.

From the other end of the street, a mule bell, and an ARRIERO comes out of the dark leading a second animal, a man in a broad hat with a whip over his shoulder and the dust of the mountain road on him to the knees.

ARRIERO

¿Los Traviesos? ¿El talabartero Juan Álvarez?

— The Traviesos? The harness-maker Juan Álvarez?

JUAN

Here.

ARRIERO

A paper from Santa Cruz, from the King's man. (He hands it over. JUAN cannot read; he gives it to VICENTE without a word.) The Lanzarote people came over on Friday. Families on the rocks at the Caleta, under San Cristóbal, with all their pots and their goats, and the old one with the white eye giving orders to people who didn't ask for them. The ship's master says the weather's turning his way. He won't wait for anyone who isn't aboard.

VICENTE reads it by the lantern. His lips move once on a word.

CATARINA

What does it say?

VICENTE

That the King's man counts the families on the eve of Passion Sunday. That we be at the Caleta by then, or our place goes to the next name.

CATARINA

San José también se fue de noche, con lo puesto.

— Saint Joseph left by night too, with what he had on his back.

A bell: Santa Ana, the first bell for the first Mass, very loud in the empty street, and then a second bell, thinner, the Dominicans of Santo Domingo up on their hill, answering it. JUAN takes the mule's halter. CATARINA lifts her hand to cross herself, for those who go and those who stay, and holds it, and does not complete the sign.

VICENTE looks once up the Calle Real, at the shuttered house with the stone shield over its door. A shutter on the upper floor, a nailed one, moves, an inch, against its nails, and is still. He turns his back to it, and walks.

The bells keep ringing. Lights down on the empty doorway, the mule dung on the stones, and, in PANCHA's arms, the copper cauldron catching the first of the light.

END OF PLAY I
